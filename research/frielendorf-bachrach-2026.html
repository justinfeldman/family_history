The Bachrach family in Frielendorf

The original registers establish Noa’s birth, his parents and the distinction between the two Hennel Spiers. They do not yet identify Jacob Bachrach’s parents. The approximate eighteenth-century birth years remain disputed. Original scans, published transcriptions and catalog-only leads are distinguished below.

The direct line to Margot

Jacob Bachrach and Beile (daughter of Noa) → Noa Bachrach and Hennel Spier of Merzhausen → Jakob Noa Bachrach → Samuel Bachrach → Margot. The later links were already in the project; this pass checks the earlier Frielendorf evidence.

Joseph’s separate branch

Joseph Bachrach, born c.1815, married Hennel Spier of Momberg on 25 April 1844. His parents are the same Jacob Bachrach and Beile Noa. His bride’s parents are Michael Spier and Sprinz née Spier. These names and the bride’s Momberg origin are visible in HHStAW 365/192, image 6, manuscript page 4, entry 12. Both this entry and Noa’s 1847 entry describe first marriages.

Joseph is Noa’s brother and a collateral relative of Margot. His wife and her parents are collateral connections, not additional direct ancestors. Their profiles therefore appear in the newer site’s extended-family area; the older site links to this separate account.

The two women are distinguished by their parents and places of origin. The records do not support the proposed explanation that one Hennel was a widow who married both brothers. Joseph’s exact birth date is not settled: the birth register gives 12 March 1815, while the household-head register appears to give 12 April 1816. The latter lists Jacob with sons Joseph, Noa and Salomon. Approximate dating avoids choosing between these entries without further evidence.

The same birth-family page also lists daughters and a younger son; the family extends beyond the two brothers. This update does not turn every listed child into a new profile or resolve later marriages and death records for them.

Evidence retained, including disagreements

“Noa” is a patronymic interpretation, not an established maiden surname. The original wording “Beile geb. Noa” is preserved alongside the display name “Beile (daughter of Noa).” Her c.1788 birth predates the Kingdom of Westphalia’s 31 March 1808 naming decree. Article 15 required a registered family name; Article 16 called for children’s numbers and ages with supporting documents. The contemporary printing in Sulamith (1808), pp. 8–9 was inspected; the Frankfurt University Library catalog identifies the source. This is relevant naming context, although birth before the decree does not by itself prove any individual patronymic or identify a father.

The already-held Neustadt death entry no. 8 of 1891 for Giedel Spier née Bachrach names her parents as Jacob Bachrach and Betti née Stern of Frielendorf. A patronymic Noa and a later hereditary surname Stern could coexist: they are not necessarily contradictory surnames. Whether this Betti and the earlier Beile are the same woman remains unresolved. Both records are retained, without asserting a second wife or a proved alias. Name-adoption records would be especially useful for testing that connection.

The local Merzhausen death scan, HHStAW 365/613, image 4, records Gütel Spier, Handelsfrau, age 44, died 16 October 1836. It does not on its own establish her maiden surname or identify her as Hennel’s mother. The earlier Tann birthplace and “Jacob” surname remain preserved in the profile notes as unconfirmed identifications.

The existing dates of death for Jacob (24 April 1846), Beile (13 January 1853), Noa (22 June 1884) and Hennel (14 April 1916) are retained with their earlier evidence labels; their original death entries were not newly checked in this pass. Likewise, Hennel’s earlier exact birth claim of 18 April 1819 is preserved alongside the newer site’s c.1818–1820 range.

Hans Isenberg’s marriage transcription is a useful finding aid, but it includes details absent from individual marriage rows: Noa’s exact day of birth, for example, is independently supported by the birth register. The original marriage row itself gives 1821. This distinction matters when assigning sources.

Jacob’s household and the Jewish life around it

A large family, visible on one page. Jacob and Beile’s original family register lists nine children over twenty years: Gehle/Gehla (1810), Ella (1812), Giedel (1814), Joseph (1815), Scheine (1818), Noa (1821), Keile (1822), Salomon (1824) and Esther (1830). Noa is the sixth entry. Spellings are working readings; Joseph’s birth differs in another register. This is a record of births, not proof that all nine survived or lived together at one time.

What “merchant” meant for Jacob. The originals call him Handelsmann, a broad word for merchant or trader. The 1817 transcription, entry 8, is more specific: Viehhandel im Kleinen, small-scale cattle trading. It supports a picture of local livestock commerce, although it establishes neither a shop nor his wealth, poverty or exact business practices. Hattenbach appears beside his name in this table: likely an origin designation, possibly birthplace or former residence. Its unlabelled columns do not prove a move in 1813.

A small market-town community. The published local history reports three Jewish households in 1744: Ephraim Höxter trading in small wares and cloth, and Jacob Höxter and Jacob Levi in cattle. It describes four annual fairs and a location between Homberg and Ziegenhain. By 1812 it counts fourteen Jewish families. This provides economic context for Jacob’s occupation; none of those earlier households is a demonstrated Bachrach ancestor.

The burden of protection payments. An archive catalog gives a particularly human example: in 1730 Salomon (Samuel) Levi of Frielendorf requested scheduled instalments for overdue protection payments because he was impoverished. Another 1736 file concerns Jacob Levi’s protection and entry payments. These are descriptions of surviving files, not transcriptions of inspected petitions. They show the kinds of financial obligations earlier Jewish residents faced; they do not establish Jacob Bachrach’s own financial position.

Worship before the familiar synagogue building. The cataloged 1817–1819 “Exzesse in der Synagoge zu Frielendorf,” HStAM 22 a 3, 511, is a promising window onto the community while Jacob was raising his family. Its title does not establish what happened, who was involved, or whether the incident was an internal dispute or outside hostility. The catalog and its representation details list only the original paper file; no public digital copy was available when checked. Copies can be requested through the Hessian State Archives reproduction service, subject to availability and a fee.

The published building history describes worship in Elias Blum’s house, documented from 1829, and subsequent conversion of part of a barn into a synagogue. The dates differ across accounts: 1834, 1835 and 1843. The 1817–1819 file therefore predates that later building story, but does not by itself identify an earlier address. The same local history dates the Jewish elementary school to 1843. These are community milestones, not evidence that Jacob held a particular religious office.

Looking for Jacob in Hattenbach

Birth-record search, 19 November 2026. The Niederaula Jewish birth register, HHStAW 365/619, which also includes Hattenbach, begins in 1820. The Hessian State Archives dates the mandatory synagogue registers in Kurhessen to 1823. No cataloged Hattenbach Jewish birth register covering 1775/76 was found, and no birth entry or contemporary source naming Jacob’s parents was identified. Older tax, protection and resident lists remain possible indirect sources; absence of a cataloged birth book does not prove no private record ever existed.

Death-register conflict. The full original Frielendorf death register, HHStAW 365/194, image 5, entry 25, records Jacob’s death on 24 April 1846 and age 60. This agrees with Hans Isenberg’s published death-index transcription. It does not record his parents. The Find a Grave memorial lists 1778 and attaches a cropped image of the death entry, but the pictured entry does not establish 1778. The two sons’ original marriage entries independently give 1776, which remains the working year; the age 60 conflicts with those entries and is treated as an error.

A closer match in published family research, but no newly verified Hattenbach birth or residence entry. The Bacharach surname project’s regional map has a Frielendorf marker reading “Jacob Bachrach, b. 1776 in Hattenbach.” Its Hattenbach marker also associates Jacob with 1776; a second Frielendorf marker gives his death year as 1846 and repeats the Hattenbach origin. The matching name, Frielendorf connection and dates make this a strong candidate for our Jacob. The map does not cite the underlying record, so its birthplace statement remains a secondary-source claim rather than proof of birth there. The map’s public data export was saved with the research files.

The project’s 19 July 2015 gathering report also describes two descendants of a Jacob Bachrach associated with Hattenbach meeting in Jerusalem. It uses 1775, one year earlier than the map and the birth year in the sons’ marriage entries. This is a practical lead to other researchers and their evidence; the recovered family chart below supplies the household connection through Noa and Beile. The project’s contact page is the next route to ask for the documentation behind those entries. No contact has been made.

The project’s older family chart identifies the same household. A Frielendorf page preserved in July 2013 links a family chart compiled by Rachel Unkefer. It begins with Jacob Bacharach, born 1776 in Hattenbach, and Bele bat NOAH, born 1788, then lists their nine children. Noah’s 14 April 1821 birth, his marriage to Hennel Spier of Merzhausen, and the sibling group establish that the chart concerns the family being researched here. No parents are shown for Jacob. The chart supports the interpretation of Beile’s name as a patronymic: bat means daughter of. It supplies no archival citations; some dates differ from the originals, including its 1828 birth year for Hennel. The original registers remain the basis for the tree’s working dates.

Wider paternal-line context. A 16 November 2009 project report describes close Y-DNA matches between a participant with Frielendorf ancestry and participants from Kestrich, alongside a match with Fellheim. The report includes a qualification about the DYS464 marker. Rachel Unkefer’s 2013 article, pp. 45–50, also includes Hattenbach in the study and discusses older connections among dispersed Bacharach families. This is context for shared paternal ancestry among the tested lines, not identification of Jacob’s parents. The participant’s documented descent would need to be compared with our family before assigning specific DNA results to this branch. The associated Frankfurt project explicitly describes some of its historical Y-charts as speculative and incomplete; they are not a proved chain from Jacob to a named medieval rabbi.

Access limit: the project’s linked searchable genealogy database returned a missing-page error during this check. The recovered family chart therefore establishes what was displayed there by 2013, not the extent of the researchers’ present private knowledge.

What the local record search established. Hattenbach is included in the Niederaula Jewish register series. The original men-and-sons table in HHStAW 365/622, digital images 2–5, was inspected without finding an identifiable Jacob Bachrach. Its 1808–1856 range includes sons’ birth dates and should not be read as a complete household census taken in 1808. All 37 images were saved; the later section concerns synagogue-seat payments. The published 1817 Niederaula household transcription likewise yielded no identifiable entry for him. Since he is already associated with Frielendorf by then, this does not disprove earlier residence in Hattenbach. The original 1817–1819 file is HStAM 33 b, 285; the checked catalog offered no public digital copy.

Earlier material survives. The Hattenbach tax-rectification volume, HStAM 49 d, Hersfeld 116, 1754–1800, is digitized. The first 84 of its 167 images were downloaded and portions surveyed, including village-description and property entries, without establishing a Bachrach connection. It has not been completely transcribed or searched. A separate 1728–1734 Jewish-resident specification, HStAM 17/2, 1109, explicitly includes Hattenbach and is digitized, but its manuscript contents remain unexamined. These are leads for earlier households, not evidence of Jacob’s parents.

The same family-project map mentions a Rabbi Seligman Bachrach associated with Hattenbach and Rotenburg, without dates or a source. No relationship to Jacob is established, and he has not been added to the tree. Hattenbach remains a tentative origin in Jacob’s profile.

Can the records take Jacob’s line further back?

Not yet. The inspected household page supplies Jacob’s sons, not his own parents. A catalog’s starting date of 1790 or 1792 is the earliest date represented in a volume; it does not demonstrate that Jacob appears there in that year or that there is a continuous eighteenth-century household census.

The published 1817 household list, entry 8, associates Jacob with Hattenbach, Beile Noa and small-scale cattle trading. It also prints 1813 in his row. The underlying manuscript and the meaning of that date need checking before calling it a settlement, marriage or admission year. The published 1828 list, entry 9, again groups the couple and their children. These are promising leads, not newly verified evidence of Jacob’s birth or parents.

Catalog details were retrieved directly from Arcinsys and saved locally. “No digital-copy link exposed” describes the checked page, not a claim that no reproduction can ever be obtained. Several keyword searches returned paginated results; this was a targeted search, not an exhaustive survey of all district holdings.

There is also a cataloged 1587 protection letter for a Jew named Jakob in Frielendorf, HStAM 17/1 no.176. It establishes early Jewish presence, but the given name and locality do not connect him genealogically to the eighteenth-century Jacob Bachrach.

The useful next sequence is to inspect the 1799–1814 marriage protocols, reconcile Jacob and Beile’s death records and the 1817/1828 household originals, and then pursue Hattenbach and district protection files with the resulting names. Jacob’s parents remain blank in the family tree.

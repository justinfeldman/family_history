August 24, 1768 · Wittelsberg

Kauffmann David and Olcke’s mortgage

Paula Baum Collection, reel 1, PDF pages 200–204. Brackets mark uncertain readings and explanations. The film counter is one higher than the PDF page number.

Page 200 · English translation

[Recorded on] page 17 in the new mortgage book. [Clerk's signature.]

I, Kauffmann David, protected Jew at Wittelsberg, and Olcke, my lawful wife, hereby declare and acknowledge, as joint debtors each liable for the whole, for ourselves and our heirs, that the surviving widow of the late Doctor [Bonell?] in Marburg, at our earnest request, has lent and advanced to us in cash 200 Reichsthaler, written out: two hundred Reichsthaler, each thaler reckoned at 48 albus, in [eight-kreuzer pieces?]. We personally received these monies before delivery of this bond and have applied them again to our evident advantage and best benefit.

We therefore not only give the said lady creditor due receipt by virtue of this instrument, and knowingly and deliberately renounce the objections that these monies were not paid to us or were not applied to our benefit, but also pledge and promise [continued].

Page 201 · English translation

We further promise to restore and repay this capital, after prior notice of [one quarter of a year?], together with any outstanding interest and costs, should any costs unexpectedly have to be incurred, in a single undivided sum in the above-mentioned currency, fully and with thanks. Until then we shall pay interest annually on the principal at the rate customary in the Empire.

So that the aforementioned lady creditor may be all the more secure for the capital lent, interest and costs, we, the debtors, pledge to her as a special mortgage:

One simple house, together with the stable and a small garden behind it, adjoining Andreas Fischer, together valued at 600 florins.

This is done cum constituto possessorio [with the debtors retaining possession subject to the creditor's security], so that [continued].

Page 202 · English translation

The creditor shall have full power and authority, with or without judicial proceedings, in the unforeseen event of nonpayment, to obtain satisfaction herself by execution against it for capital, interest and costs. We also confer on this obligation the force of a privileged instrument [an instrument permitting expedited enforcement].

Against all of this no objections, evasions or assistance of legal remedies, whatever their nature or name, shall avail us debtors. Rather, we renounce them, all and each separately. In particular, I, the female co-debtor, renounce all privileges accorded to women, namely that a woman cannot become surety or debtor for a stranger, much less for her husband; likewise [the protection of] the counter-settlement and the marriage property brought in, and in sum every legal benefit accorded to women, whatever its name, concerning which [continued].

Page 203 · English translation

I have been sufficiently and clearly instructed and informed by the local office, to the effect that, should my husband be in default on the above capital of two hundred Reichsthaler, interest and costs, I shall in that event be obliged to answer for the whole alone and to pay on his behalf. [Latin reference to the legal privilege being renounced is not securely legible.]

In witness and confirmation of the foregoing, we have not only signed this obligation with our own hands, but have also had it confirmed by the authorities. Thus done at Kirchhain, 24 August 1768.

[Official signature: Carl/… Wilhelm …, name and title partly uncertain.]

[Hebrew-script signature; not securely deciphered.] Olcke [signature or identifying endorsement; following marks uncertain].

Actum [recorded at] Kirchhain, 24 August 1768. Both the above-named debtors appeared [continued].

Page 204 · English translation

In person before the local office, acknowledged their signatures and most solemnly renounced the legal privileges available to them, as above.

[Official signature, surname uncertain.]

Ruling in the case of Olcke, surviving widow of the deceased Jew Kauffmann David of Wittelsberg, and Meier Elias of Frankenau, appointed guardian over her children, plaintiffs, against her son David Kauffmann of Wittelsberg, defendant.

1799–1800 · Wittelsberg

Etel Kaufmann’s marriage agreement

English translation

I. Preliminary marriage agreement, 23 September 1799

Image 0031 — beginning

Wittelsberg, 23 September 1799.

Be it known hereby that today a Jewish marriage agreement was concluded between Meyer Katz, legitimate son of Sussmann Katz of Grüsen, as bridegroom on the one side, and Etel Kaufmann, surviving daughter of the merchant David, deceased in Wittelsberg, as bride on the other side.

I. The conditions agreed on the bridegroom’s side are as follows:

The bridegroom’s father shall build a new dwelling house with a stable for the future married couple and shall equip them with all necessary household furnishings. In the meantime he shall take the young couple into his own residence with free food and support. The future married couple may retain their own earnings until the new house has been built and the young couple has been provided with everything stated above.

The bridegroom’s father shall provide his son with the customary clothing for holidays and workdays, on the occasion of the wedding as the contribution owed by him, together with a gold wedding ring.

Image 0032, left-hand page — continuation

The bridegroom’s father is further obliged to issue a written undertaking concerning Chalitza, described as removal of the shoe, and to transfer the independent protection privilege to his son, the bridegroom.

II. Conditions on the bride’s side.

The bride brings to the bridegroom three hundred gulden—written as 300 florins—in cash. The bride’s brother, David Kaufmann, is obliged to pay this bridal portion thirty days before the wedding.

The bride’s mother, in turn, shall provide the bed and the customary holiday and workday clothing. She shall also issue a written assurance that the bride will share equally with her other siblings in the mother’s eventual estate.

The wedding shall be held in Wittelsberg at the expense of the bride’s mother. The bridegroom’s father shall contribute two Carolin coins as assistance.

III. If, between the present day and the wedding …

Image 0038 — conclusion and court confirmation

… either party should dissolve this marriage arrangement, the party initiating the separation shall pay the other fifty gulden—written as 50 florins—as compensation.

Done at Kirchhain on 23 September 1799.

All contracting parties and their advisers appeared before the local office. After the agreement had first been read aloud and signed, they acknowledged the foregoing contract and requested that it be judicially confirmed. Their request was granted.

[Several signatures follow, some in Hebrew script. Legible Latin-script signatures include Sussmann Katz, Meyer Katz, Edel Kauffmann, and David Kauffmann; other signatures are uncertain.]

II. German rendering of the Jewish marriage contract

Image 0032, right-hand page — beginning

May good fortune spring forth and rise like the growth of a watered garden: these are the words of the covenant and contract agreed and concluded between the two parties at the time of the wedding, on the third day of the week, the fifteenth day of the month of Kislev, in the year 5561, here in the village of Wittelsberg.

The parties are Mr. Sussmann Meyer Katz of Grüsen in the Amt of Gemünden and his son, the bridegroom Meyer, on the one side; and the surviving widow of Kaufmann David, the protected Jew who died here, together with her daughter, the bride Edel, on the other side.

Before us, the undersigned witnesses, both parties bound themselves in every respect as follows.

First, the bridegroom, Meyer Sussmann Katz, married his bride Edel Kauffmann with a gold woman’s ring …

Image 0033, left-hand page — continuation

… and led her beneath the wedding canopy according to the custom of Moses and Israel. She likewise accepted the marriage in accordance with all customary forms.

Mr. Sussmann promises to purchase for the couple a proper dwelling house with which the said married couple will certainly be satisfied. If he is unable to obtain one, the said Mr. Sussmann and his wife Hanne promise that their own dwelling house shall pass to the young couple. This transfer, however, cannot occur until after the deaths of both Mr. Sussmann and his wife.

After their deaths no person, whatever name or purported basis that person may have, may assert a claim to the house. It shall fall solely and entirely to the young married couple as their property.

Image 0033, right-hand page — continuation

In return, the young married couple are obliged to allow the bridegroom’s siblings free and undisturbed residence in the parental house for as long as those siblings remain unmarried.

If the bridegroom’s father, Mr. Sussmann, should during his lifetime be prevented from purchasing a house for the young couple, the couple shall receive the interest at six percent on capital held by the resident Henrich [surname uncertain, possibly Ernst] in Grüsen until they receive a dwelling from the bridegroom’s father.

The bridegroom’s father also promises to provide the young married couple, for one full year without charge, food as good as that received by any of the other people in his household.

Image 0034, left-hand page — continuation

He has further provided his son, the bridegroom, with respectable clothing for the Sabbath, festivals, and ordinary days, and has supplied him with the gold woman’s ring in a manner appropriate to his station.

The bride’s mother, described in the manuscript as “the old Kaufmann David’s,” gave her daughter a bridal portion of three hundred gulden. She also supplied respectable clothing for the Sabbath, festivals, and other days; caps and valuables or jewelry; and a complete bed with all its accessories, everything appropriate to the bride’s station.

The bridegroom’s father obtained a Chalitza letter from all his other sons. Edel’s mother, in turn, gave her daughter a written assurance that after the mother’s death Edel was to inherit half as much as one of her sons [the clause referring to the firstborn is difficult to read].

Image 0034, right-hand page — continuation

From this time forward the married couple are obliged to live in love and unity. Neither shall conceal or reserve anything in the world from the other—neither he from her nor she from him—and both shall have equal authority over their property.

If, which may it not happen, Meyer Sussmann should treat his wife Edel in a manner that she cannot endure and she consequently has to leave him, he must provide for her support …

Image 0035, left-hand page — continuation

… by paying her each month for as long as the dispute between them continues. He must also surrender all her clothing, jewelry, and everything belonging to her personal attire.

She must, however, appear before the court having jurisdiction over them within the first thirty days. The decision of the judge shall settle the dispute. Once they have reconciled, Edel shall return to her husband’s house, and he shall return to her the remaining money, clothing, and valuables that he had retained.

The following was agreed concerning deaths.

Image 0035, right-hand page — continuation

If, which may it not happen, Meyer should die before his wife during the first year after the marriage, Edel shall take back everything she brought into the marriage, but not the money promised to her in the marriage and augmentation letter.

If, God forbid, he should die during the second year after the marriage, she shall recover what she brought into the marriage together with one half of the money promised in the marriage and augmentation letter.

If, which may it not happen, Edel should die during the first year after the marriage, Meyer must return to her heirs everything she brought into the marriage, after deducting the funeral expenses.

If she dies during the second year after the marriage, Meyer must return one half of what she brought into the marriage, after deducting the funeral expenses.

These provisions apply when the couple have no surviving offspring or bodily heirs.

Image 0036, left-hand page — continuation

If they do have surviving offspring or bodily heirs together and one spouse dies during the years already described—or if they have no bodily heirs and one spouse dies during the third or a later year after the marriage—the following applies. If the husband dies, the wife takes everything she brought into the marriage and all the money promised to her in the marriage and augmentation letter, in full satisfaction.

If the wife dies, the law of Moses and Israel applies, according to which the husband inherits the wife’s property.

It is nevertheless expressly agreed that whenever the husband dies, regardless of the time, Edel shall take her wedding ring without its value being deducted from the amount due under her marriage and augmentation letter.

All these provisions were agreed before us, the undersigned witnesses, through the grasping of the mantle and a handshake, and with legally competent witnesses. This is not to be regarded as a sham contract or merely a draft …

Image 0036, right-hand page — conclusion

… but as binding. We took possession from each party on behalf of the other by means of an object legally fit for effecting acquisition. Everything shall be confirmed and observed.

[Signatures or name marks follow, partly in Hebrew script and not reliably legible.]

III. Certification and official confirmation

Image 0037, left-hand page — certification of the German rendering

I hereby certify that this text agrees with the original word for word. Wittelsberg, 3 December 1800.

[Signature uncertain: A. Mittel/Michel Salomon]

[Further handwritten signatures in Hebrew and Latin script.]

Image 0037, lower left-hand page — official confirmation

Both parties have now personally signed this marriage contract and acknowledged its contents, and they have duly requested its confirmation. Their application for confirmation is therefore granted with full legal force insofar as the bride’s property is concerned.

With respect to the bridegroom’s property, both parties must likewise apply to his court of competent jurisdiction for joint confirmation of this marriage instrument. Dated as above.

Image 0037, right-hand page — fee and dispatch notation

No. 1020. A fee and dispatch calculation follows, including entries of 10 for the tax and 10 for expedition; the remaining abbreviated accounting notation is unclear. The matter is marked as dispatched.

August 19, 1898 · Wittelsberg

A protest at the Kirchhain cattle market

Allgemeine Zeitung des Judentums, reporting on the market of July 13.

English translation

Kirchhain, August. Under the heading “The Jews’ Revenge,” the German Farmers’ League’s paper, the Deutsche Tages-Zeitung, carries this report from here: “The town of Kirchhain has paid in an unusual way for casting a very large number of votes for the antisemite Dr. Böckel in the recent Reichstag runoff. On the thirteenth of last month Kirchhain, which had hitherto held some of the most important cattle markets in our province, held another market. In place of the usual 600 to 1,000 head, only about 100 were brought in. Most Jewish dealers stayed away from the Kirchhain market and, according to a report to the K.Ztg., attended the cattle market in Gießen instead.”

How times change! When Dr. Böckel stirred up the antisemitic movement in Hesse, so-called “Jew-free cattle markets” were established at his suggestion. After a few years they naturally failed miserably. Today people would like to make a case against the Jews when they take the same course themselves. How becoming this virtuous indignation is to the antisemites who are forever saying, “Do not buy from Jews.”

German text at Alemannia Judaica

March 31, 1808 · Wittelsberg

The Westphalian decree on Jewish worship and surnames

English translation

Royal decree of March 31, 1808, ordering the establishment of a consistory and the appointment of syndics to supervise Jewish worship. We, Jérôme Napoleon, by the grace of God and the Constitution King of Westphalia, French Prince, etc., considering that if Jews, like our other subjects, are to enjoy the free exercise of their religion, that exercise must also be subject to our supervision like the others, so that it may not conflict with legislation or with the public morality that must guide all people and make of them a single political society; that the Jews must no longer constitute a separate society within the state, but, following the example of all our other subjects, must become part of the nation whose members they are; that this union must nevertheless not lead any one of them to consider himself free of the costs of worship or debts contracted by their communities to meet those costs or discharge former burdens imposed upon them; on the report of our Minister of Justice and the Interior and after hearing our Council of State, have ordered and do order:

1. A consistory for the Jewish religion shall be established in our city of Kassel. It shall consist of a president chosen without distinction from among the rabbis or other Jews, three rabbis, two Jewish scholars and a secretary. Its members shall be proposed to our Minister of Justice and the Interior and confirmed by us.

2. When a member dies or leaves, a new member shall be appointed upon the consistory’s nomination of two candidates for each vacancy.

3. The president’s salary is fixed at three thousand francs, each rabbi’s at two thousand, each other member’s at one thousand, and the secretary’s at two thousand.

4. The consistory shall oversee: (1) everything concerning religious worship; (2) assessment, collection, administration and use of contributions and endowments for the costs of worship; (3) assessment, collection and administration of contributions and endowments for the consistory’s salary and for schools and charitable institutions maintained by Jews for children and the poor of their religion; (4) execution of measures already taken or yet to be taken to discharge the debts of the former Jewish communities.

5. Oversight of worship includes rituals or religious ordinances, services, synagogues, discipline and religious instruction. The consistory shall regulate these under government supervision and with its approval. It shall examine and supervise rabbis and Jewish teachers. They may assume office only after confirmation by our Minister of Justice and the Interior.

6. The consistory shall ensure that rabbis and teachers teach obedience to the laws at every opportunity, particularly those concerning defense of the fatherland, and teach that military service is a sacred duty during which the law releases a person from incompatible religious practices; that public prayers for us and our house are offered in every synagogue; and that rabbis do not bless marriages or pronounce divorces until shown evidence of the civil act of marriage or divorce.

7. Upon the consistory’s proposal, our Minister of Justice and the Interior shall determine the principal synagogue of each department and the number and location of subordinate synagogues.

8. Syndics shall be appointed for oversight in each department. Their number and functions shall be determined on the consistory’s proposal, and the minister shall appoint them on its nomination.

9. On the consistory’s proposal the government shall also determine how funds for the expenses named above, already assigned or to be assigned, shall be assessed, collected, administered and spent, and prescribe the means of recovery.

10. All these expenses, particularly the pay of the consistory, rabbis and teachers, maintenance and repairs of temples and synagogues, schooling of orphans and poor children, aid to the aged and infirm, and debts of former Jewish communities, shall be met from the endowments and obligations existing for each purpose. Any shortfall shall be met by proportional contributions. After ministerial approval the prefect, on the advice of the subprefects, shall declare the assessment rolls enforceable.

11. The former Jewish communities shall continue within the boundaries they had before the kingdom’s present territorial division, but shall continue as separate bodies only with respect to the debts they incurred and the obligations for which their members are liable.

12. The Jewish communities shall provide immediately for the payment of their debts. Until these have been discharged every Jew must continue to contribute to the debts, costs and burdens of the community to which he belonged before the present territorial division.

13. Every Jew settling in the kingdom must, within six weeks, register at the synagogue in whose district he takes up residence, so as to contribute to the costs of worship.

14. From May 1 of this year the mayor, or in his absence his deputy, shall record the civil status of Jews in every municipality. In agreement with the civil authority the consistory and rabbis shall ensure that Jewish families register births, marriages and deaths before those officials in accordance with the Napoleonic Code. Mayors and deputies shall follow the code and our decree of January 22 of this year in maintaining registers and recording acts.

15. Within three months of publication of this decree, all Jews shall add to the name by which they are known a surname which shall become their family’s distinguishing name. They must register it with the municipality of residence. Neither they nor their children may change it without our permission, under penalty for falsifying a name. Mayors shall ensure that they adopt neither names of towns nor names belonging to well-known families.

16. In registering these names Jews must state the number and ages of their living children. To substantiate the ages they must present certified extracts from birth registers, if these exist, or other documents previously in use among them. If these registers or documents lack authenticity, the children’s ages shall, whenever required, be verified by documents and witnesses.

17. Our Minister of Justice and the Interior is charged with carrying out this decree. Given at our royal palace at Kassel on March 31, 1808, in the second year of our reign. Signed: Jérôme Napoleon. By order of the King, the Minister and Secretary of State, Count von Fürstenstein. Certified as a true copy by the acting Minister of Justice and the Interior, Siméon.

October 1959 · Margot and her family

Bertha’s sworn declaration

English translation

Sworn Declaration

I, the undersigned Mrs. Bertha Loewinger, née Baum, hereby declare in lieu of an oath, being aware of the importance of such a declaration, as follows:

At that time I was with my parents until their emigration and was present at the dissolution of their household. My parents' home in Wittelsberg, house no. 33, had thirteen rooms, of which twelve were fully furnished. The furniture had been acquired by my parents during their marriage; they also possessed many antiques, some of which were inherited family property.

As far as I can still remember individual objects, my parents' house contained, among other things, eight fully furnished bedrooms, because we were a large family. One of these was a furnished guest room. My parents' living room contained one antique cabinet, one sofa, two armchairs, one table, chairs, pictures, one almost-new sewing machine, and a slow-burning stove.

My grandfather's living room on the first floor contained, among other things, a mahogany suite consisting of an antique cabinet, one sofa, one table, one chest of drawers, chairs, an armchair, and a double bed. I also remember that my parents had a room containing one antique cabinet with secret compartments, one large oval table, two small tables, chairs, and carpets. In addition to the kitchen furnishings, dishes, and so forth, my parents owned one horse and two wagons, and much else that I can no longer remember.

Because of the necessary emigration, my parents had to dispose of all their belongings at prices far below their value. The valuable antiques in particular brought only a fraction of their true value, and the buyers greatly exploited the conditions confronting emigrants at the time. I can give no information about the names of those buyers because, for security reasons, my father destroyed all receipts and records when he emigrated. The buyers collected the individual objects at night in order to avoid difficulties.

I estimate my parents' resulting loss at DM 15,000.

As to my parents' emigration costs, I know personally that my late brother-in-law Rudi Finsterwald and my late sister Frieda sent the money for the ship tickets to Hamburg. Because of my father's illness, my parents had great difficulties and could travel only as first-class passengers.

Brooklyn
Bertha Loewinger

Subscribed and sworn to before me on this 5th day of October, 1959. Alfred Salwitz, Notary Public, State of New York, no. 24-3437250, qualified in Kings County. Commission expires March 30, 1961. [Signature and seal.] Authentication checked at the Consulate General of the Federal Republic of Germany in New York, October 5, 1959. [Consular stamp.]

November 30, 1949 · The Torah in Providence

The congregation’s invitation

English original · transcription

Congregation Sons of Abraham, Prairie and Potters Avenues, Providence 5, Rhode Island. Rabbi Abraham Chill. Study – WI. 1-6429. Residence – HO. 1-5202. November 30, 1949.

Dear Member:
You are cordially invited to attend the dedication service and ceremony of the presentation of a Sefer Torah to our synagogue on Sunday evening, December 11, 1949, at 6:00 p.m. This Torah is 218 years old and was saved from the clutches of the Nazis by Mr. and Mrs. David Baum, who are now presenting it to our synagogue. It is probably the oldest Torah in the City of Providence. The program for the evening includes
1. A Motor Cavalcade bringing the Torah to the synagogue.
2. Maariv services chanted by Reverend Moses, father of our Rabbi.
3. Addresses by Governor John O. Pastore, Mayor Dennis J. Roberts, and prominent Rabbis of the city.
4. Buffet supper and dancing in the vestry.

There is no charge for admission and no solicitations for funds will be made. Come and bring your friends and enjoy an evening and also pay honor to the Torah which has survived the unspeakable crimes of Hitlerism.

Sincerely yours,
The Torah Committee
I. Lazarus, Chairman

December 9, 1949 · The Torah in Providence

Paula and David with the Torah binder

English original · article and photograph caption

Baums to Present Torah Sunday
Mr. and Mrs. David Baum, who came to this country as refugees from Germany in 1939, are shown with the 218-year-old Torah, thought to be the oldest in Providence, that they will present Sunday evening to the Congregation Sons of Abraham. Mrs. Baum is pointing to the embroidered cloth, said to be three or four years older than the Torah itself, which is placed around the holy scroll. Photo by Fred Kelman.

Presentation of Old Torah This Sunday
An age-old custom having to do with the making of cloth binders for every Hebrew Torah was revealed this week in connection with the 218-year-old Torah that will be presented to the Congregation Sons of Abraham Sunday evening by Mr. and Mrs. David Baum of 26 Harvard Avenue.

The custom was brought to light because of the embroidered cloth binding around the venerable scroll that is thought to be three or four years older than the Torah itself.

As explained by Rabbi Abraham Chill, when a male child was born to Jewish parents in the Germany of several hundred years ago, relatives immediately set to work embroidering his Hebrew name on a long, white cloth. The needling took a long time, usually being completed when the child was ready to enter Hebrew, or “cheder” classes, at which time the cloth was bound around a Torah in the synagogue.

In the case of the Torah that is shown here (left), so the story goes, one of Mr. Baum’s ancestors celebrated the birth of a son by commissioning the writing of a new Torah, which, when completed in 1731, was bound with the cloth that was started when the child was born. The cloth, therefore, is thought to be three or four years older than the Torah.

Remaining in the Baum family these many generations, the Torah escaped the wrath of the Nazi regime only because it was kept at home instead of in a synagogue. The Baums sent it to a son in this country in 1936, three years before they made their escape in 1939. Previously, Mr. Baum spent two and a half years in a concentration camp near Berlin. December 1949 · The Torah in Providence

David explains the inscription

English original · article and photograph caption

Two centuries old: Probably the oldest Torah in Providence will be placed in the ark at Sons of Abraham Synagogue on Dec. 11, the gift of David Baum (right), a German refugee, who is shown with Rabbi Abraham Chill examining the acrostic from which he determined the scroll’s date. Staff photo by Frank J. Farley.

218-Year-Old Torah Given To Synagogue by Refugees. Scroll Once Used in Private Chapel In Germany to Be Dedicated Dec. 11.

A Torah scroll believed to be 218 years old and once used in the private chapel of an ancestor of a German refugee now living in Providence will be dedicated and officially placed in the ark at Congregation Sons of Abraham Synagogue on Sunday, Dec. 11. The Torah, wonderfully preserved and with perfect lettering, has been given to the synagogue by Mr. and Mrs. David Baum of 26 Harvard Avenue, who came to this city in 1939 as refugees from the Nazi regime. It will be dedicated by Rabbi Abraham Chill at services set for 6:30 p.m. The ritual will include a procession of the Torah under a portable canopy preceded by clergy, and the chanting of prayers and psalms.

In an interview yesterday, with Rabbi Chill and Mrs. Baum as interpreters, Baum said that his ancestors lived in Wittelsburg, a small community belonging to the Count of Hesse. As there was no synagogue in which to hold services, the few Jewish families gathered for worship in a chapel in the Baum home. The Baum family, which was well-to-do, commissioned a scribe in Amoneburg, a small fortress community at that time, part of the holdings of the Archbishop of Mayence, to write the Torah.

From a barely readable acrostic, a favorite Jewish device in which certain letters are equated with numbers, Baum said he has ascertained that the scribe’s work was completed in 1731. The oldest record Baum has of his family is of an Elyiakim who was born in 1724. The family took the name of Baum, he said, in 1808 during the Napoleonic wars. Five or six families gathered in the Baum chapel for worship, he said, and the writing of the Torah was commissioned because Jewish law requires a Torah at all services.

The Torah preceded Mr. and Mrs. Baum to this country by three years, having been sent ahead to a son in New York in 1936. Baum said he feels it was Divine Providence which caused him to keep the scroll in his home rather than place it in a German synagogue as it would have been destroyed by the Nazis. The donor, who will observe his 80th birthday on Dec. 10, spent two and a half years in the Sachsenhausen concentration camp near Berlin before coming to Providence. Rabbi Chill said that the Torah may be used in the synagogue under ecclesiastical law for the reading of a portion of the week.

December 16, 1949 · The Torah in Providence

The dedication report

English original · article and photograph caption

218-Year-Old Torah Dedicated In New Home
The 218-year-old Torah that was presented to the Congregation Sons of Abraham Sunday by Mr. and Mrs. David Baum is shown here as it was placed in the Ark by Louis Trostonoff. A standing room throng of more than 700 witnessed the dedication of the Torah. Photo by Marcello.

Congregation Sons of Abraham this week is in possession of what is believed to be the oldest Torah in the city of Providence. The Torah, written in Germany in 1731, was formally placed in the Holy Ark Sunday following the presentation and dedication ceremonies witnessed by more than 700 persons.

Governor John O. Pastore, Rabbi Eli A. Bohnen, president of the Rabbinical Association of Rhode Island, and Rabbi Abraham Chill of Congregation Sons of Abraham, were featured speakers at the historic event.

Eighty-year-old David Baum, to whom the Torah was handed down through succeeding generations of his family, carried the Torah to the “bima” beneath a canopy borne by Mitchell Shulkin, Harry Nozick, I. Goldstein and Ted Zilman. Other Torahs were carried by Samuel Mossberg, congregation president; Michael Orzeck, chairman of the board; Morris Kirshenbaum, secretary; I. Brodsky, Hyman Shindler and David Kelman. Rabbi Chill placed a new mantle on the venerable Torah, and Isadore Lazarus placed a crown on the holy scroll.

The committee that arranged the ceremonies was headed by Lazarus and included Orzeck, Louis Trostonoff, Louis Bedrick, Charles Bresler and Herman Austern, vice president, ex-officio.

The Herald later corrected its account of the mantle. Herman Austern, the congregation’s vice president, put it on the Torah. Follow-up, December 23, p. 12
